# Train habits first, let Stockfish check

Good coaches don't start a ~500-rated adult on more chess knowledge. They start with a **thinking habit**, and an AI coach that wants to teach "organically" should do the same. The Dutch Steps Method, Dan Heisman's "Real Chess", ChessKid, Lichess Learn and Chess.com's coach-written study plans all open the same way: piece safety, counting attackers against defenders, a checks-captures-threats scan before every move, basic mates, and tactics one pattern at a time. Opening theory, strategy and deeper endgames wait until roughly 1000–1400. Data from 600-rated rapid games shows why: **36% of blunders come from missing the opponent's threat and 27% from never looking at the reply, while only 1% come from low time** ([chessstats.gg](https://chessstats.gg/stats/lichess-rating-guide)). Learning science supplies the method. A new motif gets a worked example first. After that, puzzles act as retrieval practice with immediate, explained feedback; they are spaced out, later mixed, and guidance fades as mastery grows. The largest longitudinal dataset (44,213 Chess.com players) links lessons and game reviews to about **3.6× more learning per hour than just playing**, and puzzles were the weakest of those activities ([Southwick et al. 2026](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/09567976261452568)). Human coaches review a game in a fixed order. The student first recalls what they were thinking, then the coach finds the *first* turning point and asks which step of the thinking routine was skipped. Only after that does the engine confirm. Stockfish's job is to pick the moments, fact-check every sentence and judge the student's answers, never to recite 25-move lines. The feature catalogue already holds most of this skeleton (B3–B5, C1, C4, D4, H3), but the research suggests four changes. Make self-review before the engine (B7) core. Add per-motif mastery with fading guidance, a safety-check trainer, drills for converting won positions and a tilt guardrail. Rework Woodpecker rounds (C6) and move-quality labels (B9). Replace the centipawn-based level estimate (G5) with a skill profile. One caveat runs through all of it: **almost no controlled research exists on how to teach chess itself**. These recommendations rest on practitioner consensus, general learning science and correlational platform data, so the app should measure its own results.

## Every beginner curriculum puts safety before knowledge

Strip away the branding and the major curricula agree on something surprising: below about 1000, the thing to teach is not more chess knowledge but a way of looking at the board. The **Dutch Steps Method** was developed in 1987 by teaching specialist Rob Brunia and former Dutch national coach IM Cor van Wijgerden, and it is now the official method of the Royal Dutch Chess Federation ([Han Schut](https://lichess.org/@/HanSchut/blog/the-steps-method-for-learning-and-teaching-chess/L74I4Ihq)). It runs six levels; **Step 1 covers players up to about 800 and Step 2 up to about 1400** ([chess-steps.com](https://www.chess-steps.com/)). GM Noël Studer says to add 200–300 points to those ceilings for Lichess equivalents ([Studer](https://lichess.org/@/NoelStuder/blog/the-chess-step-method-explained/QCCUDwgt)). That puts a ~500 Chess.com adult at Step 1, moving into early Step 2. The method's trademark is to "chop all tactics into little building blocks", sort every tactic into a category and give each category its own **search strategy** ([chess-steps.com](https://www.chess-steps.com/)). Each theme first appears "in its easiest appearance", and complexity rises step by step. The authors insist that workbook drilling be combined with serious games and analysis of them ([The Amazing Chess World](https://theamazingchessworld.blogspot.com/2012/10/the-steps-method_10.html)). Step 2's trainer manual covers double attack, the pin, eliminating the defence, mate in two, the knight double attack and the discovered attack, in that order ([Scribd](https://www.scribd.com/document/377544474/Trainer-Manual-2)). Adoption is wide but the evidence is thin. The only effectiveness claim is the authors' own report that children taught this way outplayed comparison groups after a year, and the comparison design is unknown ([Han Schut](https://lichess.org/@/HanSchut/blog/the-steps-method-for-learning-and-teaching-chess/L74I4Ihq)).

**Dan Heisman** contributes a diagnosis of *how* beginners think. His 1999 article describes three stages. In **Flip-Coin Chess**, a move "is played quickly and without serious thought". In **Hope Chess**, you move without checking what the opponent might threaten next. In **Real Chess**, for every candidate move you "anticipate and evaluate all your opponent's main candidate moves (especially all checks, captures, and threats)", and you do it on every move, not most of them ([ChessCafe](https://www.chesscafe.com/text/real.txt)). He later split Hope Chess into two forms. *Basic* Hope Chess is typical of advanced beginners, who "make no attempt to see if their move is safe". *Passive* Hope Chess appears at 1200–1700: players scan the board for familiar patterns and call a move safe because nothing looked dangerous. In his words, "Using tactical vision is simply not sufficient" ([Chess.com](https://www.chess.com/article/view/passive-vs-basic-hope-chess)). His study advice follows from this. Build skill rather than knowledge, play slow games, and drill basic tactics "not until you get it right, but until you NEVER get it wrong". As he puts it, "Don't be afraid of losing. Be afraid of playing a game and not learning something" ([danheisman.com](https://www.danheisman.com/improvelearn.html)). Two rules complete his routine: "Never play a bad move quickly", and do a sanity check by writing the move down and taking one fresh look before playing it ([danheisman.com](https://www.danheisman.com/thought-process-principles.html)). Unlike most coaching advice, Heisman's categories have an empirical basis. *The Improving Chess Thinker* repeats de Groot's think-aloud method with players from Class F to Expert. It catalogues errors such as Hope Chess and "hand-waving", meaning a move chosen on general principle without concrete analysis ([ChessCafe review](https://chesscafe.com/book-reviews/the-improving-chess-thinker/)). A 500 player sits between Flip-Coin and Basic Hope Chess, so the first coaching target is a habit.

The classic book courses are either too advanced for a 500 player or useful mainly as templates. **Silman's *Complete Endgame Course*** is organised by rating, and its first section (unrated to 999) covers only mating a lone king and avoiding stalemate ([Chess.com News](https://www.chess.com/news/view/endgames-are-fun)). Its philosophy is to "only give players the information they need to advance to the next level" ([Chess House](https://blog.chesshouse.com/?p=79)). **Yusupov's nine-book series** has 24 chapters per book. Each chapter introduces a theme, works through "progressively more difficult examples" and ends in exercises, and each book closes with a test ([jeremysilman.com](https://www.jeremysilman.com/book-review/boost-your-chess-1)). The first book targets players below 1500 Elo ([Desertcart](https://www.desertcart.nl/products/320743461-quality-chess-build-up-your-1-the-fundamentals)), which is too hard as a text for 500, but its chapter structure is an excellent lesson template. **De la Villa's *100 Endgames You Must Know*** is aimed at players from 2000 up to International Master ([jeremysilman.com](https://www.jeremysilman.com/book-review/100-endgames-you-must-know)).

The endgame-first debate turns out to be mostly folklore. Capablanca's famous instruction to study "the endgame before everything else" circulates without a source ([Exeter Chess Club](https://www.exeterchessclub.org.uk/content/quote006); [Chess.com forum](https://www.chess.com/forum/view/general/capablana-and-the-end-game)). Modern beginner plans reverse it. NM Robert Ramirez ranks tactics first and king-and-pawn endings third for 600–1000 players ([Chess.com](https://www.chess.com/article/view/nm-robert-ramirezs-beginner-study-guide-600-1000-elo)). Studer puts tactical motifs and mating patterns before basic endgame mates ([Next Level Chess](https://nextlevelchess.com/beginner-chess-training-done-right)). Nate Solon argues that most beginner games never reach an endgame, though he offers no statistics ([Zwischenzug](https://www.zwischenzug.gg/p/who-cares-about-the-endgame)). The practical resolution is "endgame early, but only a little". Teach elementary mates and stalemate avoidance at once, because you need them to win won games, and leave opposition and rook-endgame theory for later.

Online curricula converge on one ordering. **Lichess Learn** introduces the pieces (rook first), then capture, protection, check, escaping check and mate in one, then castling, en passant and stalemate, and finally piece values ([lila source](https://github.com/lichess-org/lila/blob/master/ui/learn/src/stage/list.ts)). **ChessKid's Queen level** is the closest public syllabus for 500–1000: **Hanging Pieces → Learning to Count → Double Attack → Fork → Pin → Skewer → Discovered Attacks & Double Checks → King and Rook Mate → Your Opponent**. "Basic Planning" doesn't appear until lesson 20 of the next level ([ChessKid](https://www.chesskid.com/learn/articles/lessons-guide-all-levels-topics)). **NM Dane Mattson's Chess.com 0–600 plan** sums itself up as "active learning". It recommends Daily games and 15|10 rapid, and teaches "King CPR" for escaping check: capture the checker, block, or run. Reflection follows a "positive sandwich" of two positives around one learning point, and Mattson adds that "the most effective training program is the one you are willing to consistently follow and enjoy" ([Chess.com](https://www.chess.com/article/view/coach-danes-beginner-improvement-guide)). **Ramirez's 600–1000 plan** names three core skills (board vision, spotting hanging pieces, basic checkmates) and wants each game reviewed "on our own first without any assistance" ([Chess.com](https://www.chess.com/article/view/nm-robert-ramirezs-beginner-study-guide-600-1000-elo)). Studer supplies the filter that ties these together: "Am I trying to learn addition before learning to count?" ([Next Level Chess](https://nextlevelchess.com/beginner-chess-training-done-right)). School chess coaching adds one technique worth borrowing for adults. *Chess Is Child's Play* ends each chapter with mini-games using a reduced set of pieces, so beginners play each new idea straight away ([Father Geek](https://fathergeek.com/?p=15944)).

| Source | Where a 500 Chess.com adult sits | What comes first | What waits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Steps Method | Step 1 into Step 2 | Mate in one, attacking and defending material, first double attacks | Pins, eliminating the defender, mate in two (Step 2); endgame strategy (Steps 5–6) |
| Heisman | Flip-Coin to Basic Hope Chess | Checks, captures, threats on every move; blunder check; slow games | Knowledge-heavy study |
| ChessKid | Queen level | Hanging pieces, counting, double attack and fork | Planning (King level, lesson 20) |
| Chess.com plans | Mattson 0–600, Ramirez 600–1000 | Board vision, hanging pieces, basic mates, King CPR | Strategy, opening theory |
| Silman endgames | Unrated–999 section | Mating a lone king, avoiding stalemate | Opposition and pawn endings (1000–1199), rook endings |
| Studer | "Beginner" | Piece interactions, opening principles, tactical motifs | Opening courses, the Woodpecker Method, "1800 material" |

## Learning science says show a new pattern, then make the learner recall it

The central finding in chess cognition is that **strength is mostly recognition**. Chase and Simon (1973) found that masters rebuilt real game positions from a brief glance far better than weaker players. The advantage vanished for randomly placed pieces, and masters recalled pieces in related groups called "chunks" ([Chase & Simon 1973](https://andymatuschak.org/prompts/Chase1973.pdf)). The common figure of ~50,000 chunks for a master is only an order-of-magnitude estimate. Later work showed that chunks are tied to specific squares, because mirror-reflected positions were recalled worse ([Gobet & Simon 1996](https://core.ac.uk/download/333533.pdf)). De Groot's think-aloud studies found that his search measures (how many moves, how deep, how fast) did not separate grandmasters from strong club players. The grandmasters simply chose the best move more often ([Exeter Chess Club](https://www.exeterchessclub.org.uk/content/psych.html)). That "no search difference" result is disputed: Holding argued stronger players do search deeper, and Gobet's replication found a difference in average search depth ([Chess.com](https://www.chess.com/article/the-cognitive-psychology-of-chess)). The core point survives the dispute: **better players think of better candidate moves**. A 500 player's store of patterns is nearly empty. The best return comes from the small set of very common configurations (loose pieces, back-rank mates, knight forks, pins), shown in realistic positions and on many different squares. Learning a pattern on one set of squares doesn't automatically carry over to others.

Recognition also has a failure mode the coach must train against. In Bilalić and colleagues' "Einstellung" study, experts who had found a familiar solution *said* they were looking for something better. Eye tracking showed their gaze stayed on the familiar idea ([LSE Research Online](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/102864)). When the familiar move was an outright blunder, though, all the experts and most of the novices avoided it ([Sheridan & Reingold 2013](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/367562)). That is good news for a 500 player, whose decisive errors are mostly outright blunders that a deliberate safety check can catch. It also means a learner who *says* "I checked" may not have, so the coach should test the habit through what the learner does, not what they report.

The best data on practice comes from rated tournament players. A reanalysis of 377 of them found that **peak serious solo study correlated r = .58 with peak rating**. Serious chess activity predicted rating far more strongly than formal instruction (**β = .62 vs .19**). Tournament play added almost nothing beyond study (**ΔR² = .006**), and formal lessons did not make study more effective. For players aged 44 and older, only serious study time and the number of chess books owned predicted rating ([Burgoyne et al. 2019](https://englelab.gatech.edu/articles/2019/Burgoyne_et_al_2019_AJP.pdf)). Practice explains only about a third of the variance in skill even after correcting for measurement error, so it is necessary but not sufficient ([BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/exploding-10000-hours-myth-its-no-guarantee-greatness)). These samples are mostly over-the-board players rated 1600+, and nothing similar exists for online adults below 1200. The direction is still clear: an AI coach's leverage lies in **directing effortful solo study**, not in replacing it with more instruction.

General learning research says *how* that study should be shaped. Dunlosky's review rates practice testing and spaced practice as the only two "high utility" study techniques ([APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)). Testing yourself beats re-studying at **g = 0.50** ([Rowland 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25150680/)), and a secondary summary reports a larger effect with feedback than without (0.73 vs 0.39) ([StudyCards](https://studycardsai.com/blog/active-recall-statistics)). The best gap between sessions grows with how long you want to remember ([Cepeda et al. 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/)). Mixing problem types beats practising one type at a time at **g = 0.42**, and the gain is largest for easily confused visual categories (**g = 0.67**). Pin, skewer and discovered attack are exactly that kind of category ([Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31556629/)). Bjork's "desirable difficulties" framework explains why spacing and mixing feel slower in practice yet produce more durable learning ([UChicago](https://dil.uchicago.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/Desirable-difficulties.pdf)). Chess maps onto this neatly: **a puzzle is retrieval practice**. Missed motifs should return at *expanding* intervals, ideally in a different position with the same motif. That is the reverse of the Woodpecker Method's *shrinking* cycles, a mismatch one critic has pointed out ([Zwischenzug](https://zwischenzug.substack.com/p/the-woodpecker-method)). Once a motif is known, practice sets should be mixed and the theme label hidden, so the learner has to recognise the pattern rather than read the label. No chess-specific controlled study has tested any of this; these are transfers from general learning research.

The most important qualification for "ask first" coaching is the **expertise reversal effect**. For newcomers to a domain, studying worked examples beats unguided problem solving. As expertise grows the advantage reverses, and gradually removing the guidance bridges the two ([Expertise reversal effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expertise_reversal_effect); [Worked-example effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worked-example_effect)). In one study of 104 students, novices given extra explanatory text did better on retention and transfer, while the effect reversed for experts ([Rey & Buchwald 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21443379/)). Most of this research uses well-structured tasks ([Nievelstein et al. 2013](https://dx.doi.org/10.1007/s11251-012-9237-2)). Tactics qualify, because a forcing line has a checkable answer, but strategy does not, so the findings transfer less certainly there. This resolves the apparent conflict between "make it hard" and "give guidance": **difficulty only helps once the learner has a basic grasp of the idea**. A brand-new motif deserves a worked example ("the knight checks the king and hits the rook at once") followed by a matching problem. Known motifs deserve retrieval.

Feedback and self-explanation close the loop. Shute's review concludes that **elaborated feedback**, which explains the what, how and why, beats a bare right/wrong verdict. Immediate feedback tends to help retention; delayed feedback may help transfer ([Shute 2008](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/shute%202008_b.pdf)). Prompting learners to explain their reasoning has a meta-analytic effect of **g = 0.55**, and **generic "why" prompts** were supported over specific ones ([BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/self-explanation-powerful-learning-technique-according-meta-analysis-64-studies); [Clearing House Unterricht](https://www.clearinghouse.edu.tum.de/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/CHU-KR-25_ENG_Bisra_2018_Selbsterklaerungen.pdf)). So puzzles get immediate, explained feedback. Games get delayed feedback that pulls the lessons together after the game, and a short "why does this work?" prompt is dropped once a motif is mastered.

## One-move blunders decide 500-level games, and structured review beats sheer volume

The numbers on where low-rated games are lost are stark. They come from a Lichess-derived analysis whose method page could not be retrieved, and Lichess and Chess.com ratings don't line up one-to-one at this level, so treat them as indicative.

| Rapid games (Lichess-derived) | 600 | 1000 |
|---|---|---|
| Blunders per 100 "live" moves | 38.7 | 23.3 |
| Blunders caused by a missed threat | 36% | 38% |
| Blunders from never looking at the reply | 27% | 24% |
| Blunders from playing too fast | 19% | 17% |
| Blunders from low time | 1% | 2% |
| Took free material when available | 62% | 77% |
| Found an available fork | 29% | 43% |
| Games lost in the opening | 19.7% | 10.8% |
| Score after reaching a +1 lead | 64% | 65% |

Source: [chessstats.gg](https://chessstats.gg/stats/lichess-rating-guide). A "live" move is one played while the game is still undecided.

Reading the table down the columns shows something important: **the causes of blunders barely change between 600 and 1000; only their frequency falls**. Getting from 600 to 1000 means making the same kinds of mistakes less often, not learning a different kind of chess. A second dataset of about 162,000 Lichess rapid games, mapped to Chess.com ratings, finds that **46% of blunders at 400–600 happen in positions the blunderer was winning**, against 26% at 1500–1700. It also finds the opening's share of blunders falling from 19.6% to 7.1% across those bands ([chessanalysis.co](https://chessanalysis.co/research/blunder-curve-blunders-by-rating-level)). A 1.5-million-game study names **hanging pieces in the opening** as the most common leak under 1000 ([FireChess](https://www.firechess.com/blog/most-played-openings-by-rating)). Adult improvers surveyed by ChessGoals agree that below 800 "rating gains come almost entirely from reducing blunders". Their top tip is "Notice that my opponent also makes moves" ([ChessGoals](https://chessgoals.com/how-to-gain-200-points-advice-from-adult-improvers/)). Heisman reaches the same conclusion from coaching: amateurs lose far more often to their own unsafe moves than by missing an attacking tactic ([Chess.com](https://www.chess.com/blog/danheisman/checking-for-safety-requires-more-than-using-tactical-vision)).

The evidence on which activities raise rating is correlational but points one way. In the 2026 *Psychological Science* study of 44,213 Chess.com players, activities aligned with deliberate practice showed a **3.61× advantage in learning efficiency over playing games**. **Puzzles were the least effective of them, roughly on par with playing** ([SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/09567976261452568)). The per-hour figures circulating online conflict and the full paper was paywalled, so effect sizes for individual activities are unverified. A study of 204 Chess.com players under 1000 found lessons were the only significant predictor of gains in *rapid*, at about +15.5 points per 60 lessons, while nothing predicted bullet gains ([Chess.com / SmarterChess](https://www.chess.com/article/view/chess-lessons-improvement-stats)). Across about 5,000 Lichess players, puzzle rating explained about half the variance in playing rating, with 100 puzzle points matching about 62 classical points ([Lichess forum](https://lichess.org/forum/general-chess-discussion/tactics-vs-game-ratings-a-statistical-analysis-5000-lichess-players)). That fits the idea that pattern knowledge matters, but it cannot show that puzzles *cause* rating gains. Timed puzzle runs correlated more strongly with blitz rating than untimed puzzle rating did (r ≈ 0.86 vs 0.68), which suggests recognition *speed* is part of the skill ([Lichess blog](https://lichess.org/@/capibarazul/blog/how-does-lichess-puzzle-storm-relates-to-blitzbullet-rating/YHjkeKJl)).

Coaches still treat tactics as the biggest study block. Heisman suggests about **50% puzzles, 30% annotated games, 15% other and 5% openings**, with problems at your own level, and ending each one by committing: "My final answer is…" ([danheisman.com](https://www.danheisman.com/lessons.html)). The two views fit together once the *type* of puzzle is specified. Easy, themed, high-volume sets build recognition at this level. ChessGoals' improvers describe a progression: piece safety and taking free material under 800, scanning for loose pieces and calculating one move ahead (including the opponent's forcing replies) at 800–1099, and deeper "calculate then commit" puzzles only at 1100–1399 ([ChessGoals](https://chessgoals.com/how-to-gain-200-points-advice-from-adult-improvers/)). Studer calls the Woodpecker Method unsuitable for beginners ([Next Level Chess](https://nextlevelchess.com/beginner-chess-training-done-right)). The most efficient puzzles appear to be the ones fused with game review. One improver reported progress only after switching from random puzzles to puzzles from their own games ([Lichess forum](https://lichess.org/forum/general-chess-discussion/tactics-vs-game-ratings-a-statistical-analysis-5000-lichess-players)). No study gives an evidence-based number of puzzles per day.

On **slow games and self-analysis**, Heisman advises about **55% play and 45% study**, with 85–90% of games at long time controls and human rather than computer opponents. He also warns that "for lower rated players intermediate time controls (10–30 min) can hinder improvement" ([danheisman.com](https://www.danheisman.com/lessons.html)), while Mattson recommends 15|10 ([Chess.com](https://www.chess.com/article/view/coach-danes-beginner-improvement-guide)). At 600, rapid games end in time trouble only 5% of the time, against 28% in blitz ([chessstats.gg](https://chessstats.gg/stats/lichess-rating-guide)). So in rapid, the 500 player's problem is skipping the safety check, not the clock. The owner's Lichess Classical and Daily games are the best places to practise the routine move by move. Because the coach may never help with a game in progress, the routine has to be learned beforehand, in training and sparring, and then applied alone.

**Openings** matter less than beginners think, but not zero. About one game in five is lost in the opening at 600 ([chessstats.gg](https://chessstats.gg/stats/lichess-rating-guide)), through hanging pieces and early traps rather than missing theory. Heisman gives openings 5% of study time ([danheisman.com](https://www.danheisman.com/lessons.html)), and Mattson says "Don't stress about memorizing openings" ([Chess.com](https://www.chess.com/article/view/coach-danes-beginner-improvement-guide)). ChessGoals' improvers mention no opening work below 1100 and first suggest a lean repertoire at 1400–1699 ([ChessGoals](https://chessgoals.com/how-to-gain-200-points-advice-from-adult-improvers/)). The choice between the Italian and the London is teaching philosophy, not evidence.

**Endgames** follow Silman's ladder: mates against a lone king first, then pawn endings and opposition at 1000–1199 ([Chess.com News](https://www.chess.com/news/view/endgames-are-fun)). So many low-rated blunders happen in winning positions, and a +1 lead converts only 64% of the time at 600. That makes **"win the won game" technique** unusually valuable.

**Annotation by a stronger player** is close to a coaching universal. Ben Finegold's whole recipe is to "play in strong tournaments… and analyze your games with a strong player" ([US Chess Champs](https://uschesschamps.com/node/567)). Tournament data says instruction, measured as *years of lessons*, has a modest effect ([Burgoyne et al. 2019](https://englelab.gatech.edu/articles/2019/Burgoyne_et_al_2019_AJP.pdf)). The online data, though, links lessons and reviews to efficient gains ([SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/09567976261452568)). The likely explanation is that lessons tied to the learner's own games beat lessons in general.

Expectations should be framed as ranges. In ChessGoals' model, an 800-rated 45-year-old studying four hours a week gained a **median of +194 in a year, with a 25th percentile of +51 and a 75th percentile of +483** ([Chess.com / SmarterChess](https://www.chess.com/blog/SmarterChess/how-many-rating-points-do-players-gain-annually)). FIDE and USCF records show over 1,000 adults gaining 200+ points after age 25 ([Chess.com / Bryant](https://www.chess.com/article/view/can-adults-improve-at-chess)).

## Strong coaches review the thinking before the moves

Coaches agree on the order of a game review, although no study has compared it with engine-first review. WGM Dina Belenkaya's version starts with the engine off: replay the game slowly, asking what you were thinking and which alternatives you considered. Then find the "point of no return", which is "Not the final mistake that lost the game, but the first mistake that put you on the path to trouble". Next, review with a coach or stronger player. Only then bring in the engine, "Not as your god, not as your guru, but as a sparring partner" ([Chess.com](https://www.chess.com/article/view/how-to-analyze-your-chess-games)). GM Roeland Pruijssers calls switching the engine on right after a game "the biggest mistake most players make". His method is to mark critical moments, analyse them yourself, compare with the engine and write **2–4 practical takeaways per game** to revisit later ([Chess.com](https://www.chess.com/blog/GMRoelandPruijssers/how-to-analyze-your-own-chess-games-correctly)). Yusupov, a pupil of Dvoretsky, sets out four steps: find where "the assessment of the position changed", ask "Why do you do these things?", look for missed ideas, and review the opening. A secondary summary also notes Dvoretsky's warning about self-serving annotations, where winners credit "diligent planning" and losers blame "chance happenings" ([Chess.com blog](https://chess.com/blog/Chessmo/analyze-your-own-games)). According to a student's write-up of his course, Studer looks only at evaluation swings of about ±2 and works out the idea behind each one ([Substack](https://nickvasquezmd.substack.com/p/analyzing-our-games)).

The defining move of a good human review is the question, and Heisman explains why. "I did not see it" can mean several different things: the student never looked, or looked and misjudged. In one of his examples, a student played 4...Nb4 to threaten a fork on c2 and never checked White's forcing reply 5.Qa4+, which forks king and knight. Many players *believe* they check for safety when they don't, so the coach must examine the thought process directly ([Chess.com](https://www.chess.com/blog/danheisman/checking-for-safety-requires-more-than-using-tactical-vision)). The table below links each kind of error to the skipped thinking step and the first question to ask. It is a design proposal built from Heisman's categories, not a published taxonomy, and it is the tag set B5 should use.

| What the engine sees | Skipped step | Heisman label | Ask first |
|---|---|---|---|
| Didn't answer a direct threat | "What does their last move do?" | Flip-Coin Chess | "What was your opponent's last move trying to do?" |
| Own move hung a piece or allowed a fork or mate | Safety check on own candidate | Basic Hope Chess | "After your move, what checks, captures or attacks did they have?" |
| "I looked and it seemed safe" | Systematic scan replaced by a glance | Passive Hope Chess | "Walk me through each capture they could make." |
| Principled-looking move fails tactically | Concrete analysis | Hand-waving | "What did you expect them to play back?" |
| Line only works if the opponent cooperates | Assumed an unforced reply | Assuming the opponent's move | "What if they don't play that?" |
| Missed winning a free piece | Scan for the opponent's loose pieces | Missed opportunity | "Were any of their pieces unprotected?" |
| Instant move, or blunder in time trouble | Time allocation | Time management | "Was this a moment to slow down?" |

Tutoring research gives the owner's Socratic preference a concrete structure. AutoTutor escalates from a **pump** (invite the student to propose a step) to a **hint** (a leading question), then a **prompt** (narrowed almost to a fill-in-the-blank), and finally an **assertion** that states the answer. Its developers note this cycle is "frequent in human tutoring" ([AutoTutor meets LLMs](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.09216)). In LLM versions, hints and prompts must not give the answer away, and the assertion must contain it ([same](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.09216)). The project's existing ladder (question → nudge → arrow → answer with the reason) already has this shape. Expertise reversal supplies the stop rules, because novices learn more from explicit explanation of new material ([Rey & Buchwald 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21443379/)). The coach should start by asking about the *student's own reasoning*: that costs the student little and tells the coach a lot. It should give the answer plainly after two failed attempts, when the student asks for it, when the idea is new to them, or when the point needs more than about two moves of calculation. Hint use needs tracking, since one review of Chess.com's Play Coach names untracked hint dependence as a weakness ([MyChessPlan](https://mychessplan.com/chesscom-play-coach-review-2026-adult-improvers-coach-audit/)). Mattson's "positive sandwich" sets the tone. The advice to review soon after the game, while the memory of what you were thinking is fresh, sets the timing ([Chess.com](https://www.chess.com/article/view/how-to-analyze-your-chess-games)).

## Explain purposes in human words and keep Stockfish behind the curtain

Human explanation narrates *purpose*. Chernev's *Logical Chess: Move by Move* gives "the reason for every single move" across 33 games and is often recommended as the first book after the basics ([New in Chess](https://newinchess.com/logical-chess-move-by-move)). Some readers find its repetition tedious ([Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/40215160)), which argues for one-sentence purposes rather than essays. Conceptual systems have levels too. Heisman places Silman's *The Amateur's Mind* at USCF 1300–1650 and *How to Reassess Your Chess* above 1650 ([Chess.com](https://www.chess.com/article/view/book-reveiw-the-amateurs-mind-turning-chess-misconceptions-into-chess-mastery-by-im-jeremy-silma)). Readers report that even 1200 players struggle with Aagaard's "where are the weaknesses?" question ([ChessChatter](https://chesschatter.substack.com/p/these-3-questions-will-help-you-play/comments)). No source maps concepts to Chess.com 500, so the ladder below is an inference from these level placements.

| Use constantly at ~500 | Introduce simply | Defer until 1200+ |
|---|---|---|
| Hanging and undefended pieces; counting attackers vs defenders; checks, captures and threats; the opponent's threat; piece values and trades; back-rank and queen mates; forks, pins, skewers | Opening principles; king safety; "which of your pieces is doing nothing?"; active vs passive pieces; trade when ahead | Silman's imbalances as a system; pawn-structure plans; prophylaxis; good vs bad bishop; space |

Research explains why raw engine output teaches poorly. Strong engines "do not predict human moves well". Maia, a network retrained on human games, matches the human's move over 52% of the time and predicts the exact "absolute howler", such as hanging a queen, more than 25% of the time ([McIlroy-Young et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.01855)). Maia-2 extends this with a single model that takes skill level into account across ratings ([Tang et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.20553)). Its maintainers note it is conditioned on rating *categories*, not calibrated to an individual ([CSSLab/maia2](https://github.com/CSSLab/maia2)), and it is unverified whether those categories reach down to ~500 Chess.com. Engines that play near-optimal moves also "prove to be inadequate partners" when a weaker player has to continue from the positions they create ([Hamade et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.05066)). That is the formal version of the owner's complaint that 25–30-move lines aren't how they learn. Translating engine knowledge into human concepts is not arbitrary, though. Human concepts such as material and king safety can be read directly from AlphaZero's internal representations ([McGrath et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9704706)). New engine concepts *were* taught to four elite grandmasters, but only after heavy filtering into teachable puzzle examples ([Schut et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.16410v1)). That translation and curation is exactly the coach's job.

Coaches define the engine's legitimate role precisely. Belenkaya warns that passively watching arrows "isn't analysis. This is engine worship". She recommends entering the lines you calculated in the game, finding the human idea behind the engine's top two or three moves, and asking "would I find this move in a game?" ([Chess.com](https://www.chess.com/article/view/how-to-analyze-your-chess-games)). Pruijssers notes that differences of 0–0.5 pawns are subtle and that automatic reviews are "often mistaken on subtle moves" ([Chess.com](https://www.chess.com/blog/GMRoelandPruijssers/how-to-analyze-your-own-chess-games-correctly)). For the coach, that gives the engine four jobs. It **chooses the moments**, using the largest drops in winning chances rather than raw centipawns, as Chess.com's rating-aware expected-points classifier already does ([Chess.com Help](https://support.chess.com/en/articles/8572705-how-are-moves-classified-what-is-a-blunder-or-brilliant-and-etc)). It **fact-checks every claim** the coach makes. It **judges the student's proposed moves** during questioning. And when the gap is small, it **prefers a simple, findable move** near the top of the list over its own first choice; that last job is an inference from the Maia work, not a tested coaching result. Centipawn numbers and long engine lines should never reach the screen. At most, one or two forcing moves can make a point visible: "if you play this, their bishop takes your knight for free."

The language-model side needs the same discipline, because fluent models invent chess facts. Kim et al. (2024) computed which chess concepts changed most with each move, fed those and the engine's evaluations to GPT-4o, and listed every attack actually present on the board so the model couldn't mention pieces that weren't there. Judges rated the output more accurate and informative than GPT-4o alone ([arXiv 2410.20811](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.20811)). The ThinkFish experiment combined GPT-4o with Stockfish and still invented "a knight on c6 that wasn't there" ([GitHub](https://github.com/ronaldsuwandi/thinkfish)). A developer who built a Stockfish-plus-Claude coach found the model "sometimes named moves that existed nowhere" and fixed it with automated checks ([DEV](https://dev.to/daniel_34e6cb821/how-i-built-a-virtual-chess-coach-stockfish-calculates-claude-explains-elevenlabs-speaks-2a0n)). Every serious builder has converged on the design the project already chose (H3, D15): **the engine and board compute the truth, the language model only phrases it, and a validator rejects any claimed move, square or piece the board doesn't confirm.**

## Adults keep improving when they rest after losses, choose their work and track process

The firmest number in adult chess psychology concerns **tilt**. A fixed-effects analysis of 11.3 million Lichess games found that after a loss the next game is played about **7 Elo weaker**. The effect grows to **16 Elo for players under 1200** and **24 Elo after five or more straight losses**, yet **a break of 30+ minutes erases it** (−0.1, not significant). After a loss, players are also *more* likely to start a new game within a minute. The author notes that tilt can't be fully separated from simply having an off day ([GitHub: chess-tilt](https://github.com/romanbreyten/chess-tilt)). The practical rule is simple: after a loss, especially two in a row, steer toward a break or a review rather than an instant rematch.

Self-determination theory gives a reasonable frame for motivation. People need **competence, autonomy and relatedness**, and competence alone doesn't build intrinsic motivation unless the person also feels in control ([Deci & Ryan 2000](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2000_DeciRyan_PIWhatWhy.pdf)). A survey of Duolingo users found autonomy a weak factor in gamified apps ([JST-UD](https://jst-ud.vn/jst-ud/article/download/10685/6973/33542)), so offering choice should be seen as respectful rather than proven to motivate. Growth-mindset messaging isn't worth building around. A meta-analysis of 63 studies found **d = 0.05 overall and d = 0.02 in the high-quality studies** ([Macnamara & Burgoyne 2023](https://login.x-mol.com/paper/1588542522804801536)). Concrete, process-based feedback, such as "you checked their captures on every move this game", is the better tool.

Streaks cut both ways. A large randomized trial on a maths platform found that highlighting streaks raised usage and modestly raised test scores, by 0.13–0.17 SD ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34173)). Yet Khan Academy retired its streak feature over motivation concerns ([Northwestern IPR](https://www.ipr.northwestern.edu/our-work/working-papers/2026/wp-26-05.html)). The claim that broken streaks durably suppress engagement circulates only secondhand ([Yu-kai Chou](https://yukaichou.com/gamification-study/master-the-art-of-streak-design-for-short-term-engagement-and-long-term-success/)), and Chessable users describe review backlogs as demotivating ([Chess.com forum](https://www.chess.com/forum/view/chess-openings/learning-with-movetrainer-vs-a-video-series)). The answer is forgiving streaks tied to a meaningful unit of work, such as one game reviewed or one mistake-puzzle explained, plus a cap on spaced-repetition backlogs.

On pacing, Heisman's 55/45 play/study split is explicitly "a rough approximation" ([danheisman.com](https://www.danheisman.com/lessons.html)). Studer argues there is no universal ratio. Decide consciously whether an hour is for fun or for training, so a plateau doesn't feel like failure ([Lichess blog](https://lichess.org/@/noelstuder/blog/chess-enjoyment-vs-training-striking-the-right-balance/Xbks4aBv)). Adult improvers stress that "energy beats volume" and recommend a protected 30-minute block when fresh. As one puts it, "It's simply impossible to improve in chess if you do your training/playing while mentally burned out" ([ChessGoals](https://chessgoals.com/how-to-gain-200-points-advice-from-adult-improvers/)). NM Todd Bryant's study of adults who made large gains found they **played a great deal, cared about chess more than their rating, and kept going through long plateaus** ([Chess.com](https://www.chess.com/article/view/can-adults-improve-at-chess)). In a study of players aged 10 to 77, practice and intelligence both mattered at every age, and there was no sign that older players benefit less from practice ([Vaci et al. 2019](https://nrl.northumbria.ac.uk/id/eprint/40761)). Rating is noisy and lags behind real skill, and a quarter of similar adults gain little in a year. So the coach should put process measures, such as hung pieces per game and how often the blunder check was used, on the main scoreboard, and give forecasts as ranges.

A curriculum from 500 upward should follow the consensus order, with the coach moving the owner on when skill shows it, not when the rating does. The bands below are approximate Chess.com rapid equivalents, because rating scales differ between sites. The "move on when" column holds design rules of thumb; no source publishes mastery cut-offs.

| Band (approx.) | Thinking and safety | Tactics and patterns | Endgames | Openings | Move on when |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| **~700–1000** (Steps 2, Ramirez 600–1000) | Routine extended to the opponent's forcing replies, one move deep | Pin, skewer, discovered attack and double check, removing the defender, mate in two; mixed sets with hidden labels | Two-rook "ladder" mate; square of the pawn; opposition; trade when ahead | About 8–10 moves per line, drilled against what 400–1000 players actually play (D6) | Opening-loss rate and in-game blunder rate near the 1000-level figures; conversion of won positions improving |
| **~1000–1300** (Steps 2–3, Silman's 1000–1199 tier) | A systematic scan to cure "Passive Hope Chess" | Calculate-then-commit puzzles; "two by two" visualisation | Key squares; simple pawn endings | Deepen the repertoire only where games show gaps | Fewer than one decisive blunder in most games |
| **~1300–1500+** | Candidate moves and simple plans | Deeper calculation | Lucena and Philidor rook endings | A lean repertoire: one White line, two Black replies (ChessGoals 1400–1699) | — |

The **dynamic level** (G5) should be a profile, not one number. The catalogue proposes average centipawn loss plus blunder rate, but counting every move inflates the numbers at low levels. One dataset that counts all moves finds blunders per game nearly flat across rating bands, while another that counts only "live" moves finds a steep decline ([chessanalysis.co](https://chessanalysis.co/research/blunder-curve-blunders-by-rating-level); [chessstats.gg](https://chessstats.gg/stats/lichess-rating-guide)). Better signals are **blunders per 100 live moves** in Rapid, Classical and Daily games, hung pieces per game, the opening-loss rate and the conversion rate of won positions. Alongside those sit how often each error type appears across games (B5 feeding G2), so the costliest habit gets taught first, and per-motif puzzle mastery (accuracy, solve time and how much hint help was needed). Rating serves only as a lagging cross-check. Per-motif mastery should drive the guidance ladder (worked example → partly worked → full problem → mixed and spaced) and the length of explanations, so the coach stops narrating motifs the owner has mastered. Bryant's improvers and the wide ChessGoals spread both suggest the profile will move in fits and plateaus, and the coach should say so up front.

## Conclusion

The owner's two instincts are right, and the research sharpens both. "Ask first, then explain" works best when "ask" means asking about *your own thinking* and about patterns already taught. For a motif the owner has never seen, expertise reversal says to show a worked example first and ask afterwards. "Don't teach me Stockfish lines" turns out to be a precise demand for the engine's best use. Its most valuable work is deciding what *not* to say: narrowing a game to one to three moments, preferring a move a human could find, and vetoing any claim the board doesn't support. Because the coach's real product is a habit, its north-star metric should be **how often blunders happen per live move, and which thinking step was skipped**. That measure is less noisy than rating and can be acted on directly.

Two tensions remain. The owner wants repertoire drilling, while the evidence says opening theory barely matters at 500. Self-determination theory argues for honouring the choice, so the compromise is a tiny repertoire focused on opening-phase safety and known traps, explained move by move. The second tension is that almost nothing about teaching chess has been tested in controlled studies, which makes this app an experiment with one participant. It should log per-motif mastery, hint use and blunder rates, and treat choices like spacing intervals and puzzle mix as settings to tune from the owner's own data, not as settled science.
