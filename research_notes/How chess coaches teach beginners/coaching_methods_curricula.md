# How Established Chess Coaching Methods and Curricula Teach Beginners and Improving Club Players (0–1500, focus under 1000)

Labels used below: **[Primary]** = the coach/author/publisher's own text; **[Review]** = a named reviewer or practitioner; **[Secondary]** = third-party summary/blog; **[Forum]** = forum/comment thread (weak evidence); **[Snippet]** = seen only in a search-result excerpt because the page itself could not be fetched (403/429). **Opinion** vs **Evidence** is flagged where it matters. Rating scales: Chess.com, Lichess, USCF and FIDE ratings are not interchangeable. Lichess usually runs a few hundred points above FIDE/USCF at the low end, and Chess.com rapid sits somewhere between. The target user (~500 Chess.com) is roughly Steps Method Step 1 to early Step 2, ChessKid "Queen" level, and NM Dane Mattson's 0–600 guide band.

## 1. The Dutch Steps Method (Brunia & van Wijgerden): steps, content, exercise structure, philosophy, evidence

### Takeaway
The Steps Method is six levels of paired teacher manuals and student workbooks. They run from learning the rules up to about 2100. Tactics are cut into very small, categorized building blocks, each with its own search strategy. Each theme is first shown "in its easiest appearance", and later steps add complexity. Large volumes of workbook exercises go alongside serious play. Adoption is wide (official KNSB method, 17+ countries), but evidence of effectiveness is almost all from practitioners and the authors themselves. No independent controlled study was found.

### Cited Findings
**Structure and levels**
- Six steps with rating ceilings: **Step 1 up to 800** (starts with how the pieces move), **Step 2 up to 1400**, **Step 3 up to 1600**, **Step 4 up to 1750**, **Step 5 up to 1900**, **Step 6 up to 2100**. Steps 5–6 add endgame strategy. Supplementary lines are **Workbooks Plus, Workbooks Extra and Workbooks Mix** [Primary] — [chess-steps.com](https://www.chess-steps.com/)
- GM Noël Studer: Step 1 ≈ up to 800 and Step 2 ≈ up to 1400. The rating intervals narrow at higher steps, and you should add about **200–300 points to get a Lichess rating**. Each level has a Manual plus a Workbook: "a very simple book (A4 size) with a lot of exercises corresponding to the Lessons explained in the Manual." Article dated May 6, 2023 [Snippet] — [Studer, "The Chess Step Method Explained" (Lichess blog)](https://lichess.org/@/NoelStuder/blog/the-chess-step-method-explained/QCCUDwgt)
- Each level pairs a student workbook with a trainer manual: "The combination of workbook and manual creates a school-like situation"; "All lessons are structured and contain clear goals." An online "Chess Tutor" and a weekly puzzle corner also exist [Primary] — [chess-steps.com](https://www.chess-steps.com/)
- "The first step deals with all the rules (laws) of the game of chess" [Snippet] — [Lichess forum: Chess Steps Method](https://lichess.org/forum/general-chess-discussion/chess-steps-method)

**What is taught (partial; no full official syllabus was retrievable)**
- Tactics get the most attention in every book. "All tactics are categorized", and each category has an associated **search strategy**. The method "chops all tactics into little building blocks", which makes problems easier to solve than unsorted ones [Primary] — [chess-steps.com](https://www.chess-steps.com/)
- Stated goals beyond calculation: develop a *feel* for tactical positions and a sense of when to be alert, e.g., spotting badly placed pieces [Primary] — [chess-steps.com](https://www.chess-steps.com/)
- Step 2 trainer-manual lesson sequence (partial) includes **Double attack (1) and (2), the Pin, Eliminating the defence, Mate in two, Double attack with the knight, Discovered attack (Lesson 11)** [Snippet] — [Scribd: Trainer Manual 2](https://www.scribd.com/document/377544474/Trainer-Manual-2)
- Step 3 material explicitly builds on "mate by a discovered or double check" from Steps 1 and 2. A related Dutch Steps-style exercise book opens with "Mate in one move", then "winning a piece in two moves" [Snippet; attribution between these two excerpt PDFs is unclear] — [boekhuis.nl excerpt A](https://download.boekhuis.nl/9789083553320_fragm-docb.pdf); [boekhuis.nl excerpt B](https://download.boekhuis.nl/9789493257535_fragm-docb.pdf)
- Steps vocabulary frames the **double attack** as 1+ attackers hitting 2 targets, where targets are **material or the king (check)** ("Attacking Targets") [Snippet] — [Han Schut (certified Steps trainer), "The Steps Method: attacking targets"](https://www.lichess.org/@/HanSchut/blog/the-steps-method-attacking-targets/0L2Mbyvw)

**Pedagogical philosophy**
- Themes are taught "first in their easiest appearance", and "with each step some complexity is added". The authors say the books should be combined with "Playing serious games" and analysis of those games. A reviewer rated about 1750 USCF scored about **85%** on Step 4 lessons and exercises and judged it right for players up to 1750 USCF [Review] — [The Amazing Chess World blog (2012)](https://theamazingchessworld.blogspot.com/2012/10/the-steps-method_10.html)
- Developed in **1987** by **Rob Brunia** (a pedagogue specialising in highly gifted children) and **IM Cor van Wijgerden** (former Dutch national coach). It is described as the official method of the Royal Dutch Chess Federation (KNSB), widely used in Europe and growing in the US [Snippet] — [Han Schut, "The Steps Method for learning and teaching chess" (Lichess)](https://lichess.org/@/HanSchut/blog/the-steps-method-for-learning-and-teaching-chess/L74I4Ihq); [MM Chess](https://www.mmchess.org/post/the-power-of-the-chess-steps-method-at-mm-chess)
- Used in 17 listed countries. The site calls itself the "World's leading teaching method in chess" but gives no data [Primary, promotional] — [chess-steps.com](https://www.chess-steps.com/)
- Studer (GM, coach) recommends the method for players **below 2000** in his Next Level Training course, calling it "proven and very well-structured" [Snippet] — [Studer Lichess blog](https://lichess.org/@/NoelStuder/blog/the-chess-step-method-explained/QCCUDwgt)

**Evidence of effectiveness**
- **Authors' own claim (not independently verified):** after one year, children taught with the method "were able to play much better chess than those children in comparison groups." The comparison design is unknown [Snippet] — [Han Schut Lichess blog](https://lichess.org/@/HanSchut/blog/the-steps-method-for-learning-and-teaching-chess/L74I4Ihq)
- **Anecdotes:** a Lichess user credits the method with a big jump in his game and in a local club's youth level ("one of our club members became Belgium's Women's champion"). FM Tex de Wit: "I still use Step 6 to step up my game right before a tournament"; "many IMs and FMs use Step 6" before tournaments [Forum/Testimonial] — [Lichess blog comments](https://lichess.org/ublog/L74I4Ihq/discuss); [chess-steps.com](https://www.chess-steps.com/)
- **Availability conflict:** a Lichess commenter says the digital version only goes up to Step 3. Studer (2023) says the series is not available digitally. Chessity has hosted Steps-based content [Forum vs Snippet] — [Lichess comments](https://lichess.org/ublog/L74I4Ihq/discuss); [Studer](https://lichess.org/@/NoelStuder/blog/the-chess-step-method-explained/QCCUDwgt); [Chessdom on Chessity](https://www.chessdom.com/steps-method-for-quality-chess-action-in-chessity/)
- Adult self-study: "as an adult improver you could work through step 1 and 2 easily by yourself and probably step 3" [Forum] — [Lichess forum](https://lichess.org/forum/general-chess-discussion/chess-steps-method)

### Inferences
- For a ~500 Chess.com adult, Steps places them at **Step 1 into early Step 2**. The core content there is mate-in-one patterns, capturing and protecting material, and the first double attacks. Pins, eliminating the defender, discovered attacks and mate-in-two come in Step 2.
- These design principles transfer directly to an AI coach: (a) one tactical theme per lesson, shown first in its simplest form; (b) a **named search strategy per theme** (e.g., "look for two undefended targets one piece can hit"), which matches the user's wish to learn patterns rather than copy engine moves; (c) many short, sorted exercises per theme before mixed ("Mix") review; (d) always pair study with serious games and self-analysis.
- The "attacking targets" framing (material vs king) is a simple teachable mental model for spotting double attacks.

### Gaps
- No complete official lesson list for Step 1 or Step 2 could be retrieved. The publisher pages (New in Chess), Han Schut's and Studer's Lichess blogs, and the Scribd manual were blocked (403) or truncated. Unverified recollection, not sourced here: Step 1 also covers mate in one, the rook/queen mates, attacking and defending material, and stalemate.
- No exercise counts per lesson, no explicit mastery threshold (e.g., "score X% before moving on") and no repetition schedule were found in primary sources.
- No independent, peer-reviewed or controlled study of the method's effectiveness was found.
- It is unclear whether the published rating ceilings are FIDE/KNSB or another scale. Studer's "+200–300 for Lichess" suggests a FIDE-like scale.

## 2. Dan Heisman: thinking process, Hope vs Real Chess, safety checks, under-1200 advice, study priorities, time management

### Takeaway
Heisman's central idea is that beginners lose mainly because of how they think, not what they know. He describes three levels: **Flip-Coin Chess → Hope Chess → Real Chess**. On every move, Real Chess means checking the opponent's possible **checks, captures and threats** in reply to each candidate move, to make sure the move is *safe*. He adds a final "sanity check" and pacing that uses nearly all the clock, spending most time on critical or tactical moves. For improvement he stresses skill over knowledge, slow games, repeated drilling of basic tactics "until you never get it wrong", and learning from every loss.

### Cited Findings
**Definitions (1999 Chess Café debut article, "The Secrets to 'Real' Chess")** [Primary]
- **Flip-Coin Chess:** "A move is played quickly and without serious thought." Almost all young beginners start here. "The winner of a game where both players are playing Flip-coin chess is almost random." — [ChessCafe real.txt](https://www.chesscafe.com/text/real.txt)
- **Hope Chess:** when you "make a move and don't look at what your opponent might threaten on his next move" and hope you can meet the threat later — [ChessCafe real.txt](https://www.chesscafe.com/text/real.txt)
- **Real Chess:** "You select candidate moves and, for each, you anticipate and evaluate all your opponent's main candidate moves (especially all checks, captures, and threats)." If a candidate allows a threat you can't meet, reject it. This is roughly a **3-ply** look-ahead done on **every** move: "you have to at least try to play correctly on every move, not just most of them." — [ChessCafe real.txt](https://www.chesscafe.com/text/real.txt)
- Beginner principles from the same piece: "Look for your opponent's checks, captures, and threats"; "If you see a good move, look for a better one"; "the more you are winning, the more you need to think defense first." He notes that in his own early tournaments he played Hope Chess and used about half an hour for a 2-hour time control; "all good chess players take their time!" — [ChessCafe real.txt](https://www.chesscafe.com/text/real.txt)

**Refinement: Basic vs Passive Hope Chess (Chess.com, updated Feb 19, 2018)** [Primary]
- Hope Chess = "consistently making moves without analyzing the opponent's possible forcing replies" (checks, captures, threats) — [Chess.com: Passive vs Basic Hope Chess](https://www.chess.com/article/view/passive-vs-basic-hope-chess)
- **Basic Hope Chess** is typical of advanced beginners and some lower intermediates: "They make moves and make no attempt to see if their move is safe" (they may answer one threat from the last move) — [same](https://www.chess.com/article/view/passive-vs-basic-hope-chess)
- **Passive Hope Chess** is typical at **1200–1700**: players think they check safety but only "peruse" visually for familiar patterns. "Using tactical vision is simply not sufficient." Systematic analysis of forcing replies is required. "A tactic will always start with a check, capture, or threat." — [same](https://www.chess.com/article/view/passive-vs-basic-hope-chess)
- Real Chess alone won't make you strong, but Hope Chess "will prevent you from becoming one." "If you wish to become a good player, you have to assume your opponent's will try to make their best moves." — [same](https://www.chess.com/article/view/passive-vs-basic-hope-chess)
- Example: after 1.Nc3 Nb4 Black has a discovered attack on d-pawn plus attack on c-pawn. Many students judged 1.Nc3 "safe" because they only scanned the board — [same](https://www.chess.com/article/view/passive-vs-basic-hope-chess)

**Thought-process principles (danheisman.com)** [Primary]
- "You can't play what you don't see" (board vision, tactical vision, visualization). Once you find a good move, look for the best. Each move is essentially a puzzle — [Thought Process Principles](https://www.danheisman.com/thought-process-principles.html)
- Make sure you can meet all the opponent's threats *before* he makes them. Otherwise it's Hope Chess. Assume the opponent finds his best move. After each opponent move, check whether it was a mistake, e.g., left a piece unguarded — [same](https://www.danheisman.com/thought-process-principles.html)
- **Blunder/sanity check:** write your move down before making it, then take a fresh look for the most obvious errors — [same](https://www.danheisman.com/thought-process-principles.html)
- **Time management:** pace yourself to use nearly all your time every game. Identify critical moves and spend more time on them. "Never play a bad move quickly" (unless short on time). The more tactical the position, the slower and more precise the analysis. Quiet positions can be played faster on principles. "Your judgment won't improve in 10 minutes, but your analysis can." It only takes one bad move to lose — [same](https://www.danheisman.com/thought-process-principles.html)
- A secondary summary of Heisman's per-move routine: **"WHY? What are all the things my opponent's move does? What are all the checks, captures and threats for both sides? … Is [the] move safe?"** A Heisman-derived "Chess Planner" lists threat scan → planning/candidates → analyze → blunder check [Secondary] — [Chess.com blog (Larceny)](https://www.chess.com/blog/Larceny/chess-thought-process); [Scribd "Chess Planner"](https://www.scribd.com/doc/203244380/Chess-Planner)

**Improvement / what to study (danheisman.com "Improve/Learn")** [Primary]
- "Your playing strength is the only thing that matters; in the long run your rating will follow." Improve by adding patterns or principles and by identifying and not repeating mistakes. Focus on "developing their skill, not increasing their knowledge." "Improvement begins at the edge of your comfort zone." — [Improve/Learn principles](https://www.danheisman.com/improvelearn.html)
- "Study tactics and then play according to the principles on this page." Drill basic tactics: "Practice not until you get it right, but until you NEVER get it wrong" (linked to "the 2000 basic tactical ideas") — [same](https://www.danheisman.com/improvelearn.html)
- Play as much as possible, **especially slow chess**, which builds board vision. Use most of your time every game, and if you won't, pick a faster time control. Play mostly opponents 100–200 points higher, but don't stop playing lower-rated ones. Basics on every move: take your time, count the material effect, check basic tactics — [same](https://www.danheisman.com/improvelearn.html)
- "Don't be afraid of losing. Be afraid of playing a game and not learning something." A game with several lessons is a "Three Rating Point" game. Expect years. Fundamentals first. "Don't play a game or even a move if you don't feel like trying your best." — [same](https://www.danheisman.com/improvelearn.html)
- Avoid repeatedly falling into opening traps: "It is especially critical not to continually fall into opening traps – or even lines that result in difficult positions" (2007 quote) [Forum quoting Heisman] — [Lichess forum](https://lichess.org/forum/general-chess-discussion/hot-take-the-notion-that-openings-dont-matter-is-hogwash?page=6)

**Books, columns and evidence base**
- Key titles: *Back to Basics: Tactics*, *Is Your Move Safe?*, *The Improving Chess Thinker*, *A Guide to Chess Improvement*, *Looking for Trouble*, *A Parent's Guide to Chess*, plus Adult Lesson Guides 1–3. Chess Café no longer serves the Novice Nook archive free [Primary] — [danheisman.com index](https://www.danheisman.com/)
- Chess.com column titles (2016–17) include "Spotting and Avoiding Tactics", "Comparing Candidate Moves", "How to Play When You're Winning Easily", "Don't Worry About Your Rating", "Is Speed Chess Good for You?" [Primary] — [Get Better at Chess](https://www.danheisman.com/get-better-at-chess.html)
- *Is Your Move Safe?* uses 150+ positions where a move looks safe but isn't, or looks risky but is best. Novice Nook won multiple Chess Journalists of America "Best Instruction" awards [Publisher] — [Mongoose Press](https://mongoosepress.com/?p=236)
- **Evidence-like basis:** *The Improving Chess Thinker* replicates de Groot's think-aloud "protocols" for players from **Class F** to Expert. Lower-rated players "often overlook features of the position that are obvious to better players". Errors catalogued include Hope Chess, "hand-waving" (choosing on principle without move-by-move analysis), unsupported assumptions about the opponent's reply, and poor time allocation. De Groot's finding is that strength differs more in recognition than in depth of calculation [Review] — [ChessCafe review](https://chesscafe.com/book-reviews/the-improving-chess-thinker/)

### Inferences
- For a 500-rated adult, Heisman would put the student in **Flip-Coin → Basic Hope Chess**. The first coaching target is a habit, not knowledge: before every move, (1) ask what the opponent's last move does, (2) list the opponent's checks, captures and threats against the intended move, (3) do a final "is it safe?" look. An AI coach can enforce this by asking the student to say their checks/captures/threats scan before revealing any evaluation.
- His distinction between *scanning for patterns* and *systematically analyzing forcing replies* explains why puzzle volume alone doesn't stop blunders. A coach should train both pattern recognition (puzzles) and the procedural safety check in real games.
- His time advice ("never play a bad move quickly"; spend time on tactical or critical moves) supports pushing a 500-rated player towards **slower time controls** (rapid 15|10 or daily) rather than blitz or bullet.

### Gaps
- I couldn't retrieve a primary Heisman text that **ranks study topics specifically for under-1200** (his *A Guide to Chess Improvement* and the Novice Nook archive are paywalled or offline). The "WHY? / checks-captures-threats / Is it safe?" multi-step wording comes from secondary summaries, not his own text.
- The exact wording of the "Is it safe?" step and his "Seven-step" or "ten-step" thought process variants could not be verified from primary sources.

## 3. Silman, Yusupov, de la Villa, and the endgame-first vs tactics-first vs openings debate

### Takeaway
Silman's *Complete Endgame Course* is built on "learn only the endings your rating needs". Its first section covers unrated–999, followed by 200-point bands. His imbalances method (*How to Reassess Your Chess*) is generally aimed at ~1400+. Yusupov's nine-book course has three levels (Fundamentals / Beyond the Basics / Mastery). Each level is one Build Up, one Boost and one Evolution book, with 24 theme-chapters per book, and it targets club players through to master. De la Villa's *100 Endgames You Must Know* is for ~2000+, and too advanced for beginners. Capablanca's famous endgame-first advice is widely quoted but unsourced online, and modern coaches mostly put tactics and safety before endgames for beginners, with only basic mates early.

### Cited Findings
**Silman — Complete Endgame Course**
- Organized by rating class. The first section covers players up to **999**, then bands of 200 points up to about 2399/2400. Classes are Unrated–999, Class D (1000–1199), Class C (1200–1399), and so on. Reviewers say you must read **all prior sections**, "even the patzer rating sections". Mate with knight and bishop is excluded as "too rare" [Review] — [Patzer's Review](https://patzersreview.blogspot.com/2023/10/the-only-endgame-book-you-need.html); [kyscg review](https://kyscg.github.io/2024/02/10/silamendgame); [Grandpatzer review](https://grandpatzerchess.blogspot.com/2007/03/silmans-complete-endgame-course-review.html); [chessbookreviews](https://chessbookreviews.blogspot.com/2010/04/review-silmans-complete-endgame-course.html)
- Philosophy: "only give players the information they need to advance to the next level", organized around "what you personally need to know so your endgame IQ is equal to your playing strength". Each chapter ends with a test. The reviewer likens it to Lev Alburt's methodology [Review, Brian Todd, Chess4Life instructor] — [Chess House blog](https://blog.chesshouse.com/?p=79)
- Early (lowest-band) material covers mating the lone king and preventing stalemate [Snippet] — [Chess.com News: Endgames Are Fun](https://www.chess.com/news/view/endgames-are-fun)

**Silman — Imbalances (*How to Reassess Your Chess*)**
- Silman's own list: "Superior minor piece, Pawn structure, Space, Material, Control of a key file, Control of a hole/weak square, Lead in development, Initiative (… 'Pushing Your Own Agenda'), King" (list possibly truncated) [Primary] — [Silman, Chess.com "How to Evaluate Chess Positions"](https://www.chess.com/article/view/how-to-evaluate-a-position)
- Target audience conflicts: a library catalogue record gives **1400–2100** (and teachers wanting a curriculum). Forum users say it's "more aimed towards advanced players", while a Goodreads reviewer calls it beginner-friendly [Catalogue vs Forum] — [Centre County Library record](https://search.centrecountylibrary.org/Record/437028/Description); [Chess.com forum](https://www.chess.com/forum/view/game-analysis/jeremy-silmans-imbalances)

**Yusupov's course (Quality Chess)**
- Nine books in **three levels × three books**: Fundamentals (Build Up 1, Boost 1, Evolution 1), Beyond the Basics (… 2), Mastery (… 3). A 10th "Revision and Exam 1" volume is sold in the bundle [Publisher/Retailer] — [Chess & Bridge: Level 1 The Fundamentals bundle](https://chess.co.uk/products/level-1-the-fundamentals-build-up-your-chess-1-boost-your-chess-1-chess-evolution-1-artur-yusupov); [10-book bundle](https://chess.co.uk/products/yusupov-complete-series-in-hardcover-10-book-bundle); [New in Chess: Fundamentals Series](https://newinchess.com/the-fundamentals-series)
- Aim: "take the student from the club level to master strength." Boost Your Chess 1 (published 4th) targets "below 1800" per Yusupov. The reviewer says it's useful up to 2000–2200. Each book has **24 chapters** and a final test. Each chapter introduces a theme, gives "progressively more difficult examples", then "exercises to solve with detailed solutions". It is "designed for self-study" and "methodically build[s] the student's knowledge from exercise to exercise". Topic mix: "Tactics, openings, fundamental endgames, calculation of variations and the rudiments of positional chess". Reviewer John Donaldson says it is "perfect" for adults with limited time [Review] — [jeremysilman.com review of Boost Your Chess 1](https://www.jeremysilman.com/book-review/boost-your-chess-1)
- Build Up Your Chess 1 is described as "24 lessons and interactive tests, tailored for players rated below Elo 1500" [Retailer] — [Desertcart listing](https://www.desertcart.nl/products/320743461-quality-chess-build-up-your-1-the-fundamentals). A Dutch bookseller describes the whole series as for roughly 1000–2200 [Retailer] — [De Beste Zet](https://www.debestezet.nl/catalog/images/Info/artur_yusupov.html)
- Boost 1 chapter examples (showing how themes interleave): The windmill; Pawn weaknesses; Back rank combinations; The 7th rank; Opening traps; Stalemate combinations; Outposts; Elementary endgames; The principles behind mobilization; Perpetual check; Mate in two moves; Coordination of the pieces; Zugzwang — [jeremysilman.com review](https://www.jeremysilman.com/book-review/boost-your-chess-1)

**De la Villa — *100 Endgames You Must Know***
- Premise: "contrary to what most amateurs believe, relatively few chess endgames one must know". De la Villa "prefers understanding to memorization", covers frequent endings (e.g., R+B vs R, but not two knights vs pawn), and has diagnostic tests at the start and end. Donaldson: "Players from 2000 up to International Master will find [it] quite useful" [Review/Publisher] — [jeremysilman.com review](https://www.jeremysilman.com/book-review/100-endgames-you-must-know); [New in Chess](https://www.newinchess.com/100-endgames-you-must-know)
- A reviewer recommends de la Villa's *100 Endgame Patterns You Must Know* **before** *100 Endgames* [Review] — [Chessentials](https://chessentials.com/100-endgame-patterns-you-must-know/). A newer, simpler *100 Basic Endgames You Must Know* also exists [Retailer] — [Chess & Bridge](https://chess.co.uk/products/100-basic-endgames-you-must-know-jesus-de-la-villa)

**The endgame-first debate**
- Capablanca (attributed): "In order to improve your game, you must study the endgame before everything else, for whereas the endings can be studied and mastered by themselves, the middle game and the opening must be studied in relation to the endgame." Quote sites give **no source**. A Chess.com user couldn't find it in *Chess Fundamentals*, though that book does open with elementary mates (K+R vs K) [Secondary/Forum] — [Exeter Chess Club quote page](https://www.exeterchessclub.org.uk/content/quote006); [Chess.com forum](https://www.chess.com/forum/view/general/capablana-and-the-end-game)
- Counter-view (Opinion, NM/IM-level coach Nate Solon, Mar 14 2021): the advice is "too pat". "Most games between beginners do not reach the endgame at all!" (no statistics given). Study what you enjoy, and treat endgame study as optional for beginners — [Zwischenzug: Who Cares About the Endgame?](https://www.zwischenzug.gg/p/who-cares-about-the-endgame)
- Modern beginner curricula place endgames *after* tactics, except for basic mates. NM Robert Ramirez (600–1000) ranks **tactics first**, then converting advantages, then K+P endgames. "Just like with endgame training, strategy and positional play shouldn't be your top priority for now." — [Chess.com Ramirez guide](https://www.chess.com/article/view/nm-robert-ramirezs-beginner-study-guide-600-1000-elo). GM Noël Studer's beginner order puts tactical motifs and mating patterns (step 6) before "basic checkmates in the endgame" (step 7) — [Next Level Chess](https://nextlevelchess.com/beginner-chess-training-done-right)

### Inferences
- Synthesis of the debate: almost every modern source teaches **elementary mates (K+Q, K+R vs K) and stalemate avoidance very early**, because you need them to win won games. Deeper endgame theory (opposition, key squares, Lucena/Philidor) comes after safety and basic tactics, at roughly 800–1400. This matches Silman's "only what your rating needs", which in effect is "endgame early, but only a little".
- For a 500-rated adult, Yusupov and de la Villa are too advanced as primary texts. Yusupov's *format* (theme intro → graded examples → exercises → test) is a good template for an AI coach's lesson units. Silman's imbalances are a 1200–1400+ topic, but a simplified version (material, king safety, development, piece activity) could serve as an early "how to evaluate" vocabulary.

### Gaps
- No full table of contents of Silman's *Complete Endgame Course* was retrieved, so the exact endings in the 0–999 and 1000–1199 sections are unconfirmed beyond "mating the lone king / avoiding stalemate".
- Whether Yusupov's tests use scoring thresholds for advancing (as recalled from the books) is unconfirmed by any fetched source.
- The primary source for the Capablanca quote (often credited to *Last Chess Lectures*) could not be verified.
- No data was found on how often games at under-1000 level actually reach an endgame.

## 4. Online and scholastic curricula: how Lichess, ChessKid, Chess.com, Chessable/Chessly and scholastic programs order topics up to ~1200

### Takeaway
The online curricula converge on one ordering. They teach the pieces one at a time, usually starting with the rook (straight-line movers first, pawn and knight later). Then come capturing and protecting, check, getting out of check, and mate in one. Special rules (castling, en passant, stalemate) follow, then piece values and counting. **Hanging pieces and counting** come before named tactics (double attack/fork → pin → skewer → discovered attack/double check). Then basic mates (K+Q, K+R), opening principles, and only later pawn endgames (opposition, key squares) and strategy.

## 5. What respected coaches recommend for players under 800/1000, and the consensus "first 1000 Elo" curriculum

### Takeaway
There is strong consensus for under 1000. The bottleneck is **blunders and board vision** (hanging pieces, missing the opponent's threat), not knowledge. Recommended work: (1) play many **slower** games and review them yourself first; (2) daily easy tactics and checkmate patterns; (3) a fixed per-move safety habit (opponent's checks/captures/threats); (4) opening *principles* only, no memorized theory; (5) basic mates, with endgame/strategy only later. Coaches warn against studying material meant for higher ratings.

### Cited Findings
**GM Noël Studer (Next Level Chess)** [Primary]
- Beginner study order: **1) Rules → 2) Piece interactions (attacks, captures, threats, checks) → 3) Strengths and weaknesses of each piece → 4) Opening principles, not theory → 5) Intro to strategy → 6) Basic tactical motifs and checkmate patterns → 7) Basic checkmates in the endgame → 8) How to learn step-by-step**. "One step builds upon another." "Learning about strategy without knowing the strengths & weaknesses of each piece doesn't make sense." — [Beginner Chess Training Done Right (≈early 2024)](https://nextlevelchess.com/beginner-chess-training-done-right)
- Avoid "studying things you would need to study at 1800 when you are 500". No opening courses or theory. He calls the Woodpecker Method unsuitable for beginners. Test: "Am I trying to learn addition before learning to count?" "The right information only helps you if you are at the right point to receive it." — [same](https://nextlevelchess.com/beginner-chess-training-done-right)
- His beginner course promise: "Learn only what you absolutely need to cross 1200 Elo." His filter for what to study: "How often will I need this skill?" and "What are the consequences if I get it wrong?" [Primary/Snippet] — [Beginner Chess Mastery course page](https://courses.nextlevelchess.blog/courses/beginner-chess-mastery); [Studer, "The chess skills that truly matter"](https://lichess.org/@/NoelStuder/blog/the-chess-skills-that-truly-matter/nlk2FwSS)

**NM Dane Mattson — Chess.com 0–600 plan** [Primary]
- Order: Beginner basics (moves, rules, castling, en passant, "King CPR") → Play (bots untimed → Daily 3-days/move → rapid **15|10 or 10 min**) → Learning (winning the game, endgame checkmates, capturing pieces, daily puzzles, "Winning with Tactics" as a stretch goal) → Strategy (opening principles, finding checkmate, the four-move checkmate, piece activity, Game Review) → Openings by principle only → alternate "Game Day" and "Learn and Solve" days — [Chess.com 0–600 guide](https://www.chess.com/article/view/coach-danes-beginner-improvement-guide)
- "If I had to summarize our study plan in two words, it would be **active learning**." Habits: in Daily games "first check what your opponent would play on their next turn" (checks, favorable captures). **King CPR** when in check: Capture the checker, block (Protect), or Run, since people often jump straight to running. "Don't just check"; "Watch your back rank". Game Review reflection as a "positive sandwich" (two positives around one learning point). "Don't stress about memorizing openings". Ignore the rating number. "The most effective training program is the one you are willing to consistently follow and enjoy." — [same](https://www.chess.com/article/view/coach-danes-beginner-improvement-guide)

**NM Robert Ramirez — Chess.com 600–1000 plan** [Primary]
- Priority order: **Tactics** (forks, pins, skewers, discovered attacks, mate in 1 and 2) → converting advantages/closing games → K+P endgames → strategy (pawn structure, weak squares, coordination, simplification) → openings (first 4–5 moves, principles: "As long as you control the center, develop your minor pieces, and castle, you will be fine") → playing/review — [Chess.com 600–1000 guide](https://www.chess.com/article/view/nm-robert-ramirezs-beginner-study-guide-600-1000-elo)
- Three core skills: **board vision** (what is attacked after every move), **identifying hanging pieces**, **basic checkmates**. He dislikes bullet ("we pick up too many bad habits") and prefers humans over bots. Ideally "review each game on our own first without any assistance" before Game Review. **"Two by two" visualization:** read two moves of a master game, visualize, then play them out, then 3×3. "The only thing I need from you is consistency over an extended period of time." — [same](https://www.chess.com/article/view/nm-robert-ramirezs-beginner-study-guide-600-1000-elo)

### Inferences
**Consensus "first 1000 Elo" curriculum** (synthesized from Studer, Mattson, Ramirez, ChessKid Queen level, Lichess Learn/Practice, Steps 1–2 and Heisman; ordering reflects majority practice):
1. **Rules fluency and piece interactions:** attack, capture, protect, check, escape check (Capture/Block/Run), mate in one, stalemate. *(Lichess Learn; ChessKid Pawn–Knight; Studer 1–2; Steps 1)*
2. **Material safety:** piece values, **hanging pieces**, **counting attackers vs defenders**, "what does the opponent's last move threaten?" *(ChessKid Queen 1–2; Ramirez core skills; Heisman)*
3. **Per-move thinking habit:** opponent's checks/captures/threats → my candidates → "is it safe?" blunder check. Use slow time controls (15|10 or daily) to practise it. *(Heisman; Mattson; Daniel King)*
4. **Elementary mates:** K+Q vs K, K+R vs K (with stalemate avoidance), back-rank mate, early-game mating patterns (scholar's mate defence). *(ChessKid Bishop/Rook/Queen-8; Lichess Practice Checkmates; Silman 0–999; Mattson)*
5. **First tactics, one at a time, simplest form first:** double attack/fork → pin → skewer → discovered attack/double check → removing the defender → mate in two. *(Steps 1–2; ChessKid Queen 3–7; Lichess Practice; Ramirez)*
6. **Opening principles, not theory:** centre, develop minor pieces, castle, don't move a piece twice or bring the queen out early without reason, plus avoiding common traps. *(Studer 4; Mattson; Ramirez; Heisman on traps)*
7. **Converting won positions:** trade when ahead, finish the game. *(Ramirez §3; ChessKid "When to Trade")*
8. **Then (≈800–1200+):** K+P basics (opposition, key squares, square of the pawn), simple strategy (piece activity, weak pawns, open files), visualization drills (Ramirez "two by two"), self-review before engine review.

**Implications for an "organic" AI coach for a 500-rated adult:**
- Diagnose by the *type* of mistake (hung piece, missed opponent threat, missed own tactic, didn't know a mate pattern) rather than by engine centipawn loss. Every curriculum above treats blunder prevention and board vision as the first gate.
- Teach each tactic as a named pattern with a **search strategy** (Steps), shown simplest-first, then in a mix. Drill to automaticity (Heisman's "until you never get it wrong").
- Have the student review each game *before* seeing engine output (Ramirez, Heisman's "learn something every game"). Use a "positive sandwich" reflection (Mattson) to support motivation.
- Hold back content meant for higher ratings (Studer's "addition before counting" test). For example, don't teach imbalances (Silman), opening theory or rook endgames before the safety habits stick.
